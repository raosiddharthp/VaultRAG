02 — Two Narratives

One broken system. Two ways to see it.

The same absent infrastructure shows up differently depending on where you sit. The business analyst sees it in a P&L. The technician feels it in their hands.

The Business Problem

Proprietary knowledge trapped in documents no system can query securely

The average mid-size manufacturer maintains between 12,000 and 40,000 pages of technical documentation. Equipment manuals. ISO-controlled SOPs. Non-conformance reports. Calibration records. Maintenance logs. Lockout/tagout procedures. Every one of these documents was written, reviewed, approved, and stored precisely so that the right person could access the right information at the right moment.

In practice, that information lives on a network drive organised by whoever set it up in 2011. Or in a binder on a shelf by the supervisor's desk. Or in the heads of the engineers who wrote it and have since retired. The documentation infrastructure is extensive. The retrieval infrastructure is non-existent.

"Over 80% of manufacturers cannot accurately calculate their true downtime costs. This visibility gap allows hidden expenses to accumulate unnoticed, making unplanned downtime one of the most underestimated threats to manufacturing profitability."
↗ iFactory, 2025

Cloud-based RAG tools — the obvious solution — are architecturally incompatible with this environment. Aerospace, automotive, and defence supply chain manufacturers routinely operate under NDA clauses, ITAR restrictions, and ISO 27001 requirements that prohibit sending proprietary process data to third-party APIs. The very tool that could solve the retrieval problem cannot be used in the environments where the problem is most severe.

The result is a permanent tax on operational efficiency — every hour a line is down, every wrong procedure applied, every near-miss generated by a technician working from memory because the right document was 40 metres and 3 minutes away.

The Human Problem

A technician who cannot afford to be wrong, and cannot afford to wait

Marcus has worked the floor for eleven years. He knows most of the machines by sound. At 07:14 on a Tuesday, the Haas CNC on Line 3 throws an E-04 fault. The line stops. His phone has no signal strong enough for an internet search. The manual is a 380-page PDF on a laptop in the supervisor's office, forty metres away, and the supervisor is in a shift briefing.

He asks Priya. Priya remembers something about the spindle cooling circuit from a training session eighteen months ago. They try the procedure she remembers. The fault clears. The line restarts at 07:31. Seventeen minutes of downtime.

At $260,000 per hour of downtime, seventeen minutes costs the facility approximately $73,700 — before rework, before the NCR, before the quality review that will follow when the same fault returns at 09:48.
↗ Aberdeen Group cost model

The fault returns at 09:48 because Priya remembered most of the procedure correctly. The part she misremembered — the torque specification on the spindle bearing re-seat — was not the sort of thing a person retains from a training session. It was on page 247 of the manual. It would have taken VaultRAG 6 seconds to retrieve it.

This is not a story about an incompetent technician. It is a story about a capable, experienced professional who was given no tool adequate to the moment. The documentation existed. The knowledge existed. The infrastructure to connect them did not.

03 — Pain Points

Six ways the current system fails every day.

These are not edge cases. They are the daily operational reality of a facility running without a knowledge retrieval system adequate to the floor.

Pain — 01
Documents exist. Retrieval does not.

Every piece of knowledge needed to run a facility is documented somewhere. The problem is never the absence of documentation — it is the absence of a system that can surface the right page, section, and step in under 10 seconds, from the floor, hands-free. Keyword search on a network drive is not that system.

18 min avg. search time · Gartner
Pain — 02
Cloud RAG is architecturally excluded

OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google API-based RAG tools require sending document content to external servers. For manufacturers operating under ITAR, NDA clauses, ISO 27001, or internal data governance policies, this is a hard architectural incompatibility — not a preference. The most visible AI tools on the market cannot be used in the environments that need them most.

ITAR · ISO 27001 · NDA exclusion
Pain — 03
Memory is not a procedure

Experienced technicians carry enormous procedural knowledge. They also misremember details under pressure, in noisy environments, under time constraint. The 23% of unplanned downtime caused by human error is not primarily caused by carelessness — it is caused by the gap between what a person remembers and what the document says. Those two things are not always the same.

23% of downtime · ABB / Plutomen
Pain — 04
The interface assumes a desk

Every document management system, every enterprise search tool, every portal-based knowledge base was built for a person sitting in front of a screen. A maintenance technician on a live factory floor has one hand on the machine, is standing in 90dB of ambient noise, and needs an answer in under 10 seconds. No existing tool was designed for that context. Not one.

Voice-first gap · Factory floor reality
Pain — 05
Wrong procedures compound, not resolve

An incorrect procedure does not always fail immediately. It often appears to work — the fault clears, the line restarts, the shift ends. The NCR surfaces days later. The root cause investigation finds the misapplied torque spec or the skipped verification step. By then, the machine has been running incorrectly for 48 hours and the damage is compounded. The most expensive errors are the ones that look like successes at the time.

33% quality problems · ASQ
Pain — 06
Safety procedures are the highest-stakes retrieval

Lockout/tagout procedures, pressure vessel de-pressurisation sequences, electrical isolation steps, hazardous material handling protocols — these are the procedures where a misremembered step is not a quality issue, it is a safety incident. They are also the procedures most likely to be needed in a high-pressure, time-compressed situation. The stakes of retrieval failure are physical, not just financial.

OSHA 29 CFR 1910.147 · LOTO

Fault appears mid-cycle. Line halts immediately. Marcus is on the floor. Phone signal is weak. The supervisor's laptop is in a briefing. He queries a colleague from memory.

Procedure reconstructed from memory and colleague recall
12 minutes. Cross-referencing between two technicians' recollections. The spindle bearing re-seat torque spec — 45 Nm — is misremembered as 40 Nm. The procedure is otherwise correct.
07:31
Fault cleared — line restarts
17 minutes of downtime. At $260,000/hr, the cost of this single incident is approximately $73,700. The line is running. The torque error is undetected.
09:48
E-04 returns — same fault, same line
The incorrectly torqued bearing has migrated under load. The fault returns, now with secondary spindle vibration. This is no longer a first-response procedure — it is a recurring incident. Maintenance now escalates to engineering. Another 34 minutes of downtime before root cause is identified.

NCR raised — quality review scheduled for Friday
Non-conformance report documents two unplanned stoppages, 51 total minutes of downtime, and an incorrect procedure application. The NCR will be reviewed in the Friday quality meeting.
